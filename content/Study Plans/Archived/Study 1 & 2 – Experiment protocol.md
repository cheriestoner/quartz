**Visual Discrimination of Vocal Production Types from Spectrograms**

## **Background and Motivation**

Speech, singing, and nonverbal vocalizations differ acoustically in systematic ways. Spectrograms encode time–frequency structure and may visually convey these differences.
This experiment aims to examine whether naïve participants can discriminate vocal production types from spectrogram images alone, and how they visually process these representations.
The results will provide:
- A baseline evaluation of spectrograms as representational tools
- Insight into visual attention strategies during crossmodal interpretation
- Foundations for future voice-visualization interfaces and/or sensory substitution systems)
## Research Questions
- **Can naive participants discriminate between speech, singing, and nonverbal vocalizations based on spectrogram images?** (study 1)
- **Which visual regions and features do participants rely on when making their decisions?** (study 2)
## Participants
- 20–30 adult participants
- No formal training in acoustics required
- Normal or corrected-to-normal vision
- Recruited via local university community
## Stimuli
- 60 audio samples:
	- 20 speech samples
	- 20 singing samples
	- 20 nonverbal vocalizations
- Duration normalized (e.g., 3–5 seconds per sample)
- Overall energy normalized
- Linear-frequency spectrograms *(following what Emmalie used)* generated from each sample
- Static spectrograms
## Apparatus
- Tobii eye-tracker + Tobii Pro Lab (behavioral research software)
- Standardized display size and viewing distance  
## Experimental Design
1. Task
	- 3-alternative forced-choice (3AFC): Identify the category of each spectrogram: speech, singing, nonverbal vocalization.
	- Confidence rating: 5-point Likert scale per trial.
2. Eye-Tracking Measures
	- Fixation count
	- Fixation locations and durations
	- Time to first fixation in predefined regions
	- Saccade patterns
	- Heatmaps per vocal category
	- Sequence of fixations (scanpaths)
3. Optional Feature Reporting
	- After each trial, participants indicate which visual features influenced their choice:
		- Horizontal bands
		- Vertical patterns
		- Irregular/noisy areas
		- Overall brightness
		- Shape/contour
		- Unsure
## Procedure
1. Welcome and informed consent
2. Brief instructions on task (no specific training on spectrogram interpretation)
3. 3 practice trials to familiarize participants with interface
4. Main experiment: 60 trials, randomized order
5. Optional post-task questionnaire regarding strategy and perceived difficulty
6. Debrief
*Estimated total duration: 20–30 minutes per participant*
## Data Collection
- Behavioral: accuracy, reaction time, confidence ratings
- Eye-tracking: fixation count, fixation duration, scanpaths, heatmaps
- Optional qualitative features reported
## Hypotheses
- Participants will perform above chance (33%) in distinguishing categories.
- Singing may be easiest to identify due to visually salient harmonic stacks.
- Speech and nonverbal vocalizations may be more confusable.
- Participants will rely on different visual features depending on vocal type.
## Expected Outcomes
- Provides baseline data on whether spectrograms convey category-level vocal information to naïve observers.
- Reveals which visual cues participants rely on, informing future visualization design.
- Can serve as a foundation for more advanced studies exploring crossmodal perception and affective interpretation.
